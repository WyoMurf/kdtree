 > On earth, given two (latitude, longitude) coordinates, what formula would be used to calculate the distance between them?                                                                                                                 
▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀
✦ To calculate the shortest distance between two points on the surface of a sphere (like Earth), you use the Haversine formula. 

  You cannot use standard Euclidean distance (the Pythagorean theorem, like d = √(Δ x² + Δ y²)) because that calculates a straight line through the Earth, rather than the curved path over its surface. The Haversine formula calculates the
  "great-circle distance."

  The Haversine Formula

  Given two points, Point 1 (lat₁, lon₁) and Point 2 (lat₂, lon₂):

  a = sin²\left((Δ lat)/(2)\right) + cos(lat₁) · cos(lat₂) · sin²\left((Δ lon)/(2)\right)

  c = 2 · atan2\left(√(a), √(1-a)\right)

  d = R · c

  Where:
   * lat and lon must be in radians (multiply your degrees by (π)/(180)).
   * Δ lat = lat₂ - lat₁
   * Δ lon = lon₂ - lon₁
   * R is the Earth's radius (mean radius is ≈ 6,371 km or ≈ 3,959 miles).
   * d is the distance between the two points (in the same units you used for R).

  Code Example (C)

  Here is how you would translate that formula into a C function:

    1 #include <math.h>
    2
    3 #define EARTH_RADIUS_KM 6371.0
    4 #define TO_RAD (M_PI / 180.0)
    5
    6 double haversine_distance(double lat1, double lon1, double lat2, double lon2) {
    7     // Convert degrees to radians
    8     double lat1_rad = lat1 * TO_RAD;
    9     double lat2_rad = lat2 * TO_RAD;
   10     double d_lat = (lat2 - lat1) * TO_RAD;
   11     double d_lon = (lon2 - lon1) * TO_RAD;
   12
   13     // Haversine formula
   14     double a = sin(d_lat / 2.0) * sin(d_lat / 2.0) +
   15                cos(lat1_rad) * cos(lat2_rad) *
   16                sin(d_lon / 2.0) * sin(d_lon / 2.0);
   17                
   18     double c = 2.0 * atan2(sqrt(a), sqrt(1.0 - a));
   19
   20     // Distance
   21     return EARTH_RADIUS_KM * c;
   22 }

  When is this not enough?
  The Haversine formula assumes the Earth is a perfect sphere. In reality, the Earth is an "oblate spheroid" (it bulges slightly at the equator). 

  For 99% of applications, Haversine is perfectly fine and has an error margin of less than 0.5%. However, if you are building something that requires extreme, sub-meter precision (like professional land surveying or missile guidance),
  you would instead use Vincenty's formulae, which are much more computationally expensive but account for the Earth's elliptical shape.

 > Out of curiosity, can you show me the Vincenty formula?                                                                                                                                                                                   
▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀
✦ The Vincenty formula is an iterative method developed by Thaddeus Vincenty in 1975 to calculate the distance between two points on the surface of an oblate spheroid (an ellipsoid). Because it accounts for the Earth's slight equatorial
  bulge, it is accurate to within 0.5 millimeters (unlike the Haversine formula, which can be off by as much as 0.5% due to assuming a perfect sphere).

  Since it's an iterative algorithm, it doesn't have a single, simple closed-form equation like Haversine. Instead, you loop through a set of equations until the result converges.

  The Mathematics

  First, we define the parameters of the WGS-84 reference ellipsoid (the standard used by GPS):
   * a: length of major axis of the ellipsoid (radius at equator) = $6378137.0$ meters
   * b: length of minor axis of the ellipsoid (radius at poles) = $6356752.314245$ meters
   * f: flattening of the ellipsoid = (a - b)/(a) ≈ (1)/(298.257223563)

  Given Point 1 (φ_1, L₁) and Point 2 (φ_2, L₂) where φ is latitude and L is longitude (in radians):

   1. Calculate the reduced latitudes (U₁, U₂):
      These map the geodetic latitude to the angle from the center of the ellipsoid.
      tan(U₁) = (1 - f) · tan(φ_1)
      tan(U₂) = (1 - f) · tan(φ_2)

   2. Define Initial Variables:
      L = L₂ - L₁ (difference in longitude)
      λ = L (initial guess for the difference in longitude on the auxiliary sphere)

   3. Iterate until λ converges (change in λ is very small, typically < 10⁻¹²):
       * sin(σ) = √(\left(cos(U₂) · sin(λ)\right)² + \left(cos(U₁) · sin(U₂) - sin(U₁) · cos(U₂) · cos(λ)\right)²)
       * cos(σ) = sin(U₁) · sin(U₂) + cos(U₁) · cos(U₂) · cos(λ)
       * σ = arctan2(sin(σ), cos(σ)) (angular distance on the sphere)
       * sin(α) = (cos(U₁) · cos(U₂) · sin(λ))/(sin(σ)) (azimuth of the geodesic at the equator)
       * cos²(α) = 1 - sin²(α)
       * cos(2σ_m) = cos(σ) - (2 · sin(U₁) · sin(U₂))/(cos²(α))
       * C = (f)/(16) · cos²(α) · \left[4 + f · (4 - 3 · cos²(α))\right]
       * λ_{new} = L + (1 - C) · f · sin(α) · \left{σ + C · sin(σ) · \left[cos(2σ_m) + C · cos(σ) · (-1 + 2 · cos²(2σ_m))\right]\right}

   4. Once Converged, Calculate Distance (s):
       * u² = cos²(α) · (a² - b²)/(b²)
       * A = 1 + (u²)/(16384) · \left{4096 + u² · [-768 + u² · (320 - 175 · u²)]\right}
       * B = (u²)/(1024) · \left{256 + u² · [-128 + u² · (74 - 47 · u²)]\right}
       * Δσ = B · sin(σ) · \left{cos(2σ_m) + (B)/(4) · \left[cos(σ) · (-1 + 2 · cos²(2σ_m)) - (B)/(6) · cos(2σ_m) · (-3 + 4 · sin²(σ)) · (-3 + 4 · cos²(2σ_m))\right]\right}
       * s = b · A · (σ - Δσ) (This is your final distance in meters)

  Code Example (C)

  As you can see, it is vastly more mathematically intense than Haversine:

    1 #include <math.h>
    2
    3 #define TO_RAD (M_PI / 180.0)
    4
    5 double vincenty_distance(double lat1, double lon1, double lat2, double lon2) {
    6     // WGS-84 ellipsoid parameters
    7     double a = 6378137.0, b = 6356752.314245, f = 1 / 298.257223563;
    8     
    9     double L = (lon2 - lon1) * TO_RAD;
   10     double U1 = atan((1 - f) * tan(lat1 * TO_RAD));
   11     double U2 = atan((1 - f) * tan(lat2 * TO_RAD));
   12     
   13     double sinU1 = sin(U1), cosU1 = cos(U1);
   14     double sinU2 = sin(U2), cosU2 = cos(U2);
   15     
   16     double lambda = L, lambdaP = 2 * M_PI;
   17     double iterLimit = 100;
   18     
   19     double sinLambda = 0.0, cosLambda = 0.0, sinSigma = 0.0, cosSigma = 0.0;
   20     double sigma = 0.0, sinAlpha = 0.0, cosSqAlpha = 0.0, cos2SigmaM = 0.0;
   21
   22     while (fabs(lambda - lambdaP) > 1e-12 && --iterLimit > 0) {
   23         sinLambda = sin(lambda);
   24         cosLambda = cos(lambda);
   25         
   26         sinSigma = sqrt((cosU2 * sinLambda) * (cosU2 * sinLambda) + 
   27                         (cosU1 * sinU2 - sinU1 * cosU2 * cosLambda) * 
   28                         (cosU1 * sinU2 - sinU1 * cosU2 * cosLambda));
   29         
   30         if (sinSigma == 0) return 0.0; // Co-incident points
   31         
   32         cosSigma = sinU1 * sinU2 + cosU1 * cosU2 * cosLambda;
   33         sigma = atan2(sinSigma, cosSigma);
   34         sinAlpha = cosU1 * cosU2 * sinLambda / sinSigma;
   35         cosSqAlpha = 1 - sinAlpha * sinAlpha;
   36         
   37         // Handle equatorial line edge case
   38         cos2SigmaM = cosSqAlpha == 0 ? 0 : cosSigma - 2 * sinU1 * sinU2 / cosSqAlpha;
   39         
   40         double C = f / 16 * cosSqAlpha * (4 + f * (4 - 3 * cosSqAlpha));
   41         lambdaP = lambda;
   42         lambda = L + (1 - C) * f * sinAlpha * 
   43                  (sigma + C * sinSigma * (cos2SigmaM + C * cosSigma * (-1 + 2 * cos2SigmaM * cos2SigmaM)));
   44     }
   45     
   46     if (iterLimit == 0) return -1.0; // Formula failed to converge (nearly antipodal points)
   47     
   48     double uSq = cosSqAlpha * (a * a - b * b) / (b * b);
   49     double A = 1 + uSq / 16384 * (4096 + uSq * (-768 + uSq * (320 - 175 * uSq)));
   50     double B = uSq / 1024 * (256 + uSq * (-128 + uSq * (74 - 47 * uSq)));
   51     double deltaSigma = B * sinSigma * (cos2SigmaM + B / 4 * (cosSigma * (-1 + 2 * cos2SigmaM * cos2SigmaM) - 
   52                         B / 6 * cos2SigmaM * (-3 + 4 * sinSigma * sinSigma) * (-3 + 4 * cos2SigmaM * cos2SigmaM)));
   53     
   54     // Distance in meters
   55     return b * A * (sigma - deltaSigma);
   56 }

  The "Antipodal" Problem
  One famous limitation of Vincenty's formula is that it can completely fail to converge (creating an infinite loop) if the two points are nearly exactly on opposite sides of the Earth (antipodal). If you look at the C code above, the
  iterLimit acts as a circuit breaker to prevent the program from freezing if given antipodal coordinates!

✦ Calculating the distance between two astronomical coordinates depends on exactly what you mean by "distance." 

  In astronomy, we generally use the Equatorial Coordinate System, which maps the sky onto a celestial sphere surrounding Earth. The coordinates are:
   1. Right Ascension (α or RA): The astronomical equivalent of longitude. It is usually measured in hours, minutes, and seconds (0h to 24h), where 24h = 360 degrees.
   2. Declination (δ or Dec): The astronomical equivalent of latitude. It is measured in degrees, from +90° (North Celestial Pole) to -90° (South Celestial Pole).

  Because the universe is 3-dimensional, we calculate distance in two totally different ways:

  ---

  Scenario A: Angular Distance (Apparent separation in the sky)
  If you look up at the night sky and want to know how far apart two stars appear to be from your perspective on Earth, you calculate their angular separation. You do not need to know how far away the stars actually are from Earth; you
  treat them as if they are painted on the inside of a hollow sphere.

  Because the celestial sphere is considered a perfect sphere, we can use a slightly modified version of the Spherical Law of Cosines (or the Haversine formula).

  Given Star 1 (α_1, δ_1) and Star 2 (α_2, δ_2):

  θ = arccos\Big(sin(δ_1)sin(δ_2) + cos(δ_1)cos(δ_2)cos(α_1 - α_2)\Big)

  Where:
   * α and δ must be converted to radians. 
       * To convert RA to degrees, multiply by 15 (since 360° / 24h = 15°/h). Then multiply by (π)/(180) for radians.
   * θ is the angular separation in radians. To convert back to degrees, multiply by (180)/(π).

  Note: For stars that are extremely close together, the Law of Cosines suffers from rounding errors in floating-point math, so astronomers often use the Haversine formula instead, exactly as we did for Earth.

  ---

  Scenario B: True 3D Spatial Distance (Parsecs / Lightyears)
  If you want to know the actual physical distance between two stars in space, you need their 3D coordinates. To get this, you need a third variable: Distance from Earth (r). 

  Astronomers usually determine r using parallax measurements (like those from the Gaia space telescope), where r = (1)/(p) (p being parallax in arcseconds, giving r in parsecs).

  Once you have (α_1, δ_1, r₁) and (α_2, δ_2, r₂), you convert these spherical coordinates into standard 3D Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ with Earth at the origin $(0,0,0)$:

  For each star:
  x = r · cos(δ) · cos(α)
  y = r · cos(δ) · sin(α)
  z = r · sin(δ)

  Now that you have (x₁, y₁, z₁) and (x₂, y₂, z₂), you can use the standard 3D Euclidean distance formula (the 3D Pythagorean theorem) to find the straight-line distance between them:

  d = √((x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)² + (z₂ - z₁)²)

  If your original r values were in lightyears, d will be in lightyears. If they were in parsecs, d will be in parsecs.

  Fun Fact for KD-Trees!
  This 3D Euclidean distance (d = √(Δ x² + Δ y² + Δ z²)) is exactly what your 3d/kd.c implementation calculates in the box_dist and kd_dist_sq functions! If you converted a catalog of stars from (RA, Dec, Distance) into Cartesian
  $(x,y,z)$ coordinates, you could feed them directly into the 3D KD-Tree we just consolidated to rapidly perform nearest-neighbor searches of the Milky Way galaxy.

✦ Stellar parallax is the fundamental method astronomers use to measure distances to nearby stars. It relies on simple geometry and trigonometry, taking advantage of the Earth's orbit around the Sun.

  Here is a breakdown of how it works and the math behind it.

  The Concept of Parallax

  Hold your finger in front of your face and close your left eye. Now open your left eye and close your right eye. Your finger appears to "jump" relative to the background. This apparent shift in position due to a change in the
  observer's viewpoint is called parallax.

  Astronomers do the exact same thing with stars, but instead of using two eyes, they use the Earth's orbit:
   1. They take a picture of a target star against the background of very distant, essentially stationary stars (like galaxies or quasars). Let's say they do this in January.
   2. They wait six months until July. The Earth has now traveled to the exact opposite side of its orbit around the Sun.
   3. They take another picture of the same target star.
   4. Because the Earth has moved, the target star appears to have shifted slightly against the background stars.

  The Geometry and the "Parsec"

  The distance between the Earth in January and the Earth in July is the diameter of Earth's orbit. However, astronomers use the radius of Earth's orbit (the distance from the Earth to the Sun) as the baseline for their triangle. 

  This distance is exactly 1 Astronomical Unit (AU).

  When we measure the apparent shift of the star over six months, we divide that total angle by 2 to get the parallax angle (p). This creates a right triangle where:
   * The base of the triangle is the distance from Earth to the Sun (1 AU).
   * The angle opposite the base is the parallax angle (p).
   * The long side of the triangle is the distance from the Sun to the star (d).

  Using basic trigonometry:
  tan(p) = (1 AU)/(d)

  Because the stars are so incredibly far away, the angle p is microscopically small. For very small angles, tan(p) ≈ p (when p is in radians). Therefore, the formula simplifies to:
  d = (1 AU)/(p)

  To make the math incredibly easy, astronomers invented a unit of distance called the parsec (a portmanteau of "parallax second"). 
   * A parsec is defined as the distance at which a star would have a parallax angle of exactly 1 arcsecond.
   * (For reference, 1 degree = 60 arcminutes = 3600 arcseconds. So 1 arcsecond is (1)/(3600) of a degree).

  The Formula

  Because of how the parsec is defined, the relationship between parallax and distance becomes the simplest formula in astrophysics:

  d = (1)/(p)

  Where:
   * d is the distance to the star in parsecs (pc).
   * p is the measured parallax angle in arcseconds (").

  (Note: 1 parsec is equal to about 3.26 lightyears, or 3.086 × 10¹³ kilometers).

  Example Calculation
  Proxima Centauri is the closest star to our solar system. The Gaia space telescope measured its parallax angle to be 0.768 arcseconds.

  d = (1)/(0.768)
  d = 1.302 parsecs

  To convert that to lightyears:
  1.302 pc × 3.26 ly/pc = 4.24 lightyears

  The Limitations of Parallax
  The further away a star is, the smaller its parallax angle becomes. 
   * A star 10 parsecs away has a parallax of 0.1 arcseconds.
   * A star 1,000 parsecs away has a parallax of 0.001 arcseconds.

  Eventually, the angle becomes so infinitesimally small that even our best space telescopes (like Gaia or Hubble) cannot resolve the shift against the background noise. Currently, Gaia can measure parallax accurately out to about 10,000
  parsecs (roughly 30,000 lightyears), which covers a large chunk of our Milky Way galaxy, but is useless for measuring distances to other galaxies. For galaxies, astronomers must rely on "standard candles," like Cepheid variable stars
  or Type Ia supernovae.

✦ A standard candle is an astronomical object with a known, predictable intrinsic brightness (luminosity). 

  The concept relies on the inverse-square law of light: as light spreads out spherically from a source, its apparent brightness decreases proportionally to the square of the distance. If you know exactly how bright an object actually is
  (Absolute Magnitude, M), and you measure how bright it appears from Earth (Apparent Magnitude, m), you can calculate its exact distance.

  The relationship is defined by the Distance Modulus formula:
  d = 10^{(m - M + 5)/(5)}
  (Where d is the distance in parsecs).

  Because parallax fails beyond ~10,000 parsecs, astronomers rely on two primary standard candles to measure the universe:

  1. Cepheid Variable Stars
   * What they are: Extremely luminous, unstable stars that physically pulsate—expanding and contracting, growing hotter and cooler—in a regular, repeating cycle that lasts anywhere from a few days to months.
   * How they work: In 1908, Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered the Period-Luminosity Relation (now called the Leavitt Law). She found a direct, mathematical relationship between a Cepheid's pulsation period and its intrinsic brightness:
     the longer the pulse period, the brighter the star.
   * The Process: 
     1. Observe a distant galaxy and locate a Cepheid variable star.
     2. Measure the time it takes to complete one pulse cycle (e.g., 30 days).
     3. Use the Leavitt Law to determine its Absolute Magnitude (M).
     4. Measure its Apparent Magnitude (m) from Earth.
     5. Plug m and M into the distance modulus formula.
   * Range: Effective out to about 100 million light-years. Edwin Hubble used Cepheids in 1923 to prove that the Andromeda "nebula" was actually an entirely separate galaxy.

  2. Type Ia Supernovae
   * What they are: The catastrophic thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf star. This occurs in a binary star system where a dense white dwarf is gravitationally siphoning gas off a companion star.
   * How they work: White dwarfs are supported by electron degeneracy pressure, which has a strict physical limit known as the Chandrasekhar limit (approximately 1.44 times the mass of our Sun). The moment the white dwarf siphons enough
     matter to cross this exact mass threshold, it collapses and detonates. 
   * The Process: Because every Type Ia supernova explodes at the exact same mass limit, the physics of the explosion are nearly identical every time. Consequently, they all reach a highly predictable peak intrinsic brightness (an
     Absolute Magnitude of roughly -19.3, which is 5 billion times brighter than our Sun). By measuring the apparent peak brightness of the explosion from Earth, astronomers can determine the distance.
   * Range: Because they are so intensely bright, they can be seen halfway across the observable universe (up to 10+ billion light-years). Type Ia supernovae were the standard candles used in 1998 to discover that the expansion of the
     universe is accelerating (Dark Energy).

  The Cosmic Distance Ladder
  These methods rely on each other to form the "Cosmic Distance Ladder":
   1. Radar/Kepler's Laws measure the Earth-Sun baseline (1 AU).
   2. The 1 AU baseline allows Parallax to measure the distance to nearby Cepheids.
   3. The nearby Cepheids calibrate the Leavitt Law, allowing us to measure distances to nearby galaxies.
   4. Nearby galaxies happen to occasionally host Type Ia Supernovae, which allows us to calibrate the exact peak brightness of the explosion.
   5. We then use Type Ia Supernovae to measure the edge of the observable universe.

✦ Yes, absolutely! FITS (Flexible Image Transport System) is the international standard data format for astronomy. Because it is an open standard maintained by NASA and the IAU, there is massive open-source support across almost every
  programming language.

  Since you are working with C, Rust, Go, and Julia for your KD-Tree, here are the best open-source FITS readers for those environments, plus the industry-standard Python approach.

  1. C / C++ (The Industry Standard Backbone)
  CFITSIO
  This is the foundational open-source C library developed by NASA's HEASARC (High Energy Astrophysics Science Archive Research Center). Almost every other language's FITS library (including Rust and Julia) is actually just a wrapper
  around this C library.
   * Link: https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/fitsio/ (https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/fitsio/)
   * Usage: It is highly optimized and can handle massive files (like the Gaia catalog) by reading them directly from disk in chunks without loading the entire file into RAM.

  2. Julia
  FITSIO.jl
  Since we just worked on your Julia KD-Tree, this is the perfect library if you want to parse the Gaia catalog directly into your Julia structs. It is a very clean wrapper around the NASA C library.
   * Link: https://github.com/JuliaAstro/FITSIO.jl (https://github.com/JuliaAstro/FITSIO.jl)
   * Usage:

   1   using FITSIO
   2   f = FITS("gaia_catalog.fits")
   3   # FITS files are split into Header-Data Units (HDUs). 
   4   # Catalogs are usually in HDU 2 (Table data).
   5   header = read_header(f[2]) 
   6   ra = read(f[2], "ra")   # Reads the Right Ascension column
   7   dec = read(f[2], "dec") # Reads the Declination column

  3. Rust
  fitsio
  A safe Rust wrapper around the CFITSIO library. 
   * Link: https://crates.io/crates/fitsio (https://crates.io/crates/fitsio)
   * Usage:

   1   use fitsio::FitsFile;
   2   let mut f = FitsFile::open("gaia_catalog.fits").unwrap();
   3   let hdu = f.hdu(1).unwrap();
   4   let ra: Vec<f64> = hdu.read_col(&mut f, "ra").unwrap();

  4. Go
  FITS
  There is a native, pure-Go implementation (no C-bindings required) written by the astrogo organization.
   * Link: https://github.com/astrogo/fitsio (https://github.com/astrogo/fitsio)
   * Usage: 

   1   import "github.com/astrogo/fitsio"
   2   // ... Open file, extract binary table, read columns.

  5. Python (The Easiest for Data Exploration)
  Astropy
  If you just want to poke around the Gaia catalog, filter it, or plot it before feeding it to your KD-Tree, Python's astropy library is the undisputed king of astronomical data processing.
   * Link: https://www.astropy.org/ (https://www.astropy.org/)
   * Usage:

    1   from astropy.io import fits
    2   from astropy.table import Table
    3
    4   # Open the file
    5   hdul = fits.open('gaia_catalog.fits')
    6   hdul.info() # Prints what is inside the file
    7
    8   # Extract the table data
    9   gaia_data = Table(hdul[1].data)
   10   
   11   # Print the RA and Dec
   12   print(gaia_data['ra'])
   13   print(gaia_data['dec'])

  Cosmological Redshift and Distance: Provide a concise explanation of how redshift is used to judge astronomical distances, expanding on the user's cosmological questions.


✦ Yes, absolutely! In fact, for the most distant objects in the universe (like quasars and early galaxies), redshift is the only way we can judge their distances. 

  This relies on a concept known as Cosmological Redshift and a mathematical rule called Hubble's Law.

  Here is how it works:

  1. What is Redshift?
  When an object emitting light moves away from you, the light waves get "stretched out" relative to you. Because longer wavelengths of visible light correspond to the color red, this stretching causes the object's light to shift toward
  the red end of the spectrum. (This is the exact same physics as the Doppler Effect, which causes an ambulance siren to drop in pitch as it drives away from you).

  Astronomers measure this by looking at the absorption lines in a galaxy's spectrum (barcodes created by elements like Hydrogen absorbing specific wavelengths of light). By seeing how far these "barcodes" have shifted toward the red
  compared to a laboratory on Earth, they can calculate the exact speed the galaxy is moving away from us.

  2. Hubble's Law
  In 1929, Edwin Hubble made a profound discovery using those "standard candles" (Cepheid variables) we talked about earlier. He measured the distance to various galaxies using standard candles, and then measured their redshift. 

  He found a strict, linear correlation: The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. 

  This happens because the fabric of space itself is expanding. If you imagine baking a loaf of raisin bread, as the dough expands in the oven, every raisin moves away from every other raisin. The raisins furthest apart have the most
  dough expanding between them, so they move apart the fastest.

  The relationship is defined by Hubble's Law:
  v = H₀ · d

  Where:
   * v is the recessional velocity (speed moving away, calculated directly from the redshift).
   * H₀ is the Hubble Constant (the rate at which the universe is expanding, currently estimated around 70  km/s/Mpc).
   * d is the distance to the galaxy.

  3. How to Calculate Distance
  Because H₀ is a known constant, and v is easily calculated by looking at the redshift of the light, you can trivially solve for distance:

  d = (v)/(H₀)

  If a galaxy is moving away from us at 7,000  km/s, and we assume H₀ is $70$, then the galaxy is 100  Megaparsecs away (about 326 million lightyears).

  The "z" Value
  In astronomy, redshift is denoted by the letter z. 
   * A redshift of $z=0$ means the object is right next to us.
   * A redshift of $z=1$ means the universe has doubled in size since that light was emitted, placing the object billions of lightyears away.
   * The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) recently discovered galaxies with a redshift of $z > 13$, meaning we are seeing them as they existed just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang!

  Summary of the Cosmic Distance Ladder
   1. Radar: Solar system (meters/AU)
   2. Parallax: Nearby stars (up to ~30,000 lightyears)
   3. Cepheid Variables: Nearby galaxies (up to ~100 million lightyears)
   4. Type Ia Supernovae: Distant galaxies (up to ~10 billion lightyears)
   5. Redshift (Hubble's Law): The edge of the observable universe (beyond 10 billion lightyears)

✦ When you see parallax defined in the Gaia catalog (like Gaia DR2 or DR3), it is accompanied by several other fields because the Gaia satellite doesn't just measure a single static position; it calculates a complex mathematical model of
  the star's movement across the sky over years.

  If a star is too faint, or Gaia didn't get enough good looks at it to figure out its orbit, Gaia drops back to a "2-parameter solution" (just Right Ascension and Declination). That is why parallax is often missing (NaN) for many stars
  in the file.

  When Gaia can calculate parallax, it uses a "5-parameter" or "6-parameter" solution, which populates the following extra fields.

  Do you need them to better define the parallax value?
  For basic distances: No. If you just want a rough distance to a bright star, you only need the parallax field. You just do d = (1000)/(p) (where p is in milliarcseconds and d is in parsecs).

  For accurate scientific distances: Yes, you absolutely need them. Here is what they mean and how they are used:

  1. The Uncertainty Fields (Crucial)
   * parallax_error: The formal mathematical uncertainty of the measurement.
   * parallax_over_error: This is the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). 
       * Why you need it: If this number is less than 5, you cannot use the simple $d = 1000/p$ formula. The math breaks down and gives heavily biased distances. For low SNR stars, astronomers have to use complex Bayesian statistics to
         guess the distance based on models of the Milky Way.

  2. The Quality Fields (Crucial)
   * ruwe (Renormalised Unit Weight Error): This is arguably the most important field next to parallax itself.
       * Why you need it: It tells you how well Gaia's math actually fit the star's movement. If ruwe > 1.4, it usually means the star is wobbling because it is part of a binary star system, and the parallax value is likely wrong. You
         should filter out any stars with high ruwe.
   * astrometric_params_solved: A number indicating what kind of math was used. (31 means a standard 5-parameter solution, 95 means a 6-parameter solution for stars missing color data).

  3. The Correlation Fields (Advanced)
   * ra_parallax_corr, dec_parallax_corr, parallax_pmra_corr, etc.
   * Why you need them (or don't): Because Gaia measures a star spinning through space in 3D, its proper motion (speed across the sky) and its parallax (wobble due to Earth's orbit) are mathematically entangled. These fields tell
     statisticians how strongly one measurement influenced the other. You can completely ignore these unless you are doing heavy error-propagation modeling.

  The "Zero-Point" Correction (The Hidden Requirement)
  One final thing to note: Gaia DR3 parallaxes actually have a known hardware/software glitch. Almost all of them are slightly "off" by a tiny fraction of a milliarcsecond (known as the Zero-Point Bias). 

  To get the true parallax, you actually need other fields in the FITS file:
   1. The star's brightness (phot_g_mean_mag)
   2. The star's color (nu_eff_used_in_astrometry or pseudocolour)
   3. The star's position (ra, dec)

  Astronomers pass these specific fields into a Python library called gaiadr3-zeropoint, which spits out a tiny correction number to subtract from the main parallax field. 

  Summary for your KD-Tree: If you are feeding Gaia data into your KD-Tree, filter out anything where parallax is NaN, filter out anything where ruwe > 1.4, and ideally filter out anything where parallax_over_error < 5. That will leave
  you with a highly accurate dataset of stars!

✦ Yes, you can absolutely tell what kind of star it is, but it depends on which table/FITS file you downloaded from the Gaia archive!

  The main gaia_source table provides astrometry (positions and motions) and basic photometry (brightness in different color bands). To classify a star, astronomers look at its Astrophysical Parameters, which are contained in a separate,
  companion table in Data Release 3 (DR3) called astrophysical_parameters.

  If you merge the gaia_source table with the astrophysical_parameters table (they share the same source_id), you get access to several incredible fields that tell you exactly what kind of star it is:

  1. The Simplest Way: The Spectral Type Field
  If you just want the classic "O, B, A, F, G, K, M" classification, there is a string field exactly for this:
   * spectraltype_esphs: This field provides a predicted spectral class tag for roughly 217 million stars in the catalog. 

  2. The Physical Way: Thermodynamics and Gravity (Recommended)
  Astronomers rarely rely on the string tags because they are just rough categories. Instead, they classify stars using three continuous physical measurements (derived from the star's light spectrum). These fields are heavily populated
  in DR3:

   * teff_gspphot (Effective Temperature): Given in Kelvin. For example, our Sun is ~5,778 K. If you see a star with a T_{eff} of 30,000 K, you know it's a massive, hot, blue 'O-type' star. If it's 3,000 K, it's a cool, red 'M-type'
     dwarf.
   * logg_gspphot (Surface Gravity): This is the crucial field to tell the size and stage of the star. 
       * A value around ~4.5 means it is a standard "Main Sequence" star (like our Sun).
       * A value around ~1.0 to ~2.0 means the star has ballooned into a Red Giant or Supergiant. (Because it expanded, its surface gravity is much weaker).
       * A value around ~8.0 means it is a crushed, super-dense White Dwarf.
   * mh_gspphot (Metallicity): Tells you what the star is made of compared to the Sun. A very negative number means the star is extremely old (formed before the universe was seeded with heavy elements by supernovae).

  3. The "Is it even a star?" Way: DSC Probabilities
  Sometimes you don't know if a point of light is a star, a galaxy, or a quasar. The Discrete Source Classifier (DSC) provides probabilities (0.0 to 1.0) for what the object is:
   * classprob_dsc_star: Probability it is a single star.
   * classprob_dsc_whitedwarf: Probability it is a white dwarf.
   * classprob_dsc_binarystar: Probability it is a binary system.
   * (There are also fields for classprob_dsc_quasar and classprob_dsc_galaxy).

  4. The Evolutionary Stage Field
  Finally, Gaia includes a module called FLAME that predicts exactly what stage of life the star is in:
   * evolstage_flame: This is a numeric code that tells you if the star is on the main sequence, in the subgiant phase, or has reached the red giant branch.

  How to use this in practice:
  If you download the astrophysical_parameters FITS files and read them using one of the viewers (like TOPCAT) or libraries (like FITSIO.jl), the standard way to classify stars is to plot a Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) Diagram. 

  You simply plot teff_gspphot (Temperature) on the X-axis (reversed, so hot is on the left) and the star's Absolute Magnitude (calculated from its apparent brightness and parallax distance) on the Y-axis. The stars will naturally
  cluster into visually distinct lines and branches representing Main Sequence stars, White Dwarfs, and Red Giants!

✦ When dealing with astronomical datasets as massive as Gaia DR3 (which contains over 1.8 billion sources), the choice of format comes down to a trade-off between disk space (compression) and read speed (parsing). 

  Here is the breakdown of how FITS, ECSV, and compression algorithms compare, and what you should do.

  1. Disk Space (Compression)
  ECSV (Extended Comma-Separated Values) is a plain-text ASCII format. Text is a terribly inefficient way to store numbers. Storing the number -123.456789 takes 11 bytes in ECSV (one byte per character), whereas in a FITS Binary Table,
  it takes exactly 4 or 8 bytes as a native float32 or float64.

   * Raw ECSV vs. Raw FITS: A raw FITS binary table will almost always be significantly smaller (often 50% smaller) than a raw ECSV file simply because binary encoding is denser than text.
   * Applying Gzip/Bzip2: Text files compress exceptionally well. If you gzip or bzip2 an ECSV file, it will shrink drastically (often by 70-80%). However, if you convert the data to a FITS Binary Table and then gzip it (.fits.gz), it
     will be the smallest possible file size. 

  Winner for disk space: .fits.gz (Gzipped FITS).

  2. Read Speed & CPU Overhead (The Real Bottleneck)
  This is where the format truly matters for your KD-Tree project.

  If you keep the data as ECSV (even if gzipped), your program has to decompress the text stream, search for commas to split the columns, and then run string-to-float conversions (like atof() in C or parse(Float64, ...) in Julia) on
  billions of fields. This is incredibly CPU-intensive and painfully slow.

  A FITS Binary Table, on the other hand, stores the data exactly as it looks in your computer's RAM. 
   * There is zero parsing required. 
   * There are no commas to search for. 
   * You don't convert strings to floats. 
   * C, Rust, and Julia can simply "memory map" (mmap) the FITS file or copy the raw bytes directly into your structs or arrays almost instantly. 

  Even better, the standard FITS libraries (like CFITSIO in C, or FITSIO.jl in Julia) have built-in, transparent gzip support. If you give them a .fits.gz file, they will decompress it on the fly into binary arrays much faster than you
  could ever parse a text CSV.

  The Recommended Workflow for Gaia DR3
  The official Gaia archive distributes DR3 data as highly compressed .csv.gz files to save them bandwidth on downloads. You should not try to feed these directly into your KD-Tree every time you run your program. 

  Instead, do this:
   1. Download the .csv.gz (or .ecsv.gz) files from the Gaia archive.

What is a nuclear RTG generator?

A Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG), also known as a radioisotope power system (RPS), is a type of nuclear battery that generates electricity from the heat released by the radioactive decay of isotopes like plutonium-238. ￼ It operates using the Seebeck effect, where an array of thermocouples converts the temperature difference between the hot radioactive fuel and a cold heat sink directly into electrical current. ￼

Because RTGs have no moving parts, they are highly reliable and maintenance-free, making them ideal for spacecraft traveling far from the Sun (where solar panels are ineffective) and for remote terrestrial facilities. ￼ Common missions powered by RTGs include the Voyager probes, Cassini, New Horizons, and the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers. ￼ Unlike nuclear reactors, RTGs do not rely on controlled fission chain reactions; instead, power output steadily decreases over decades based on the isotope's half-life. ￼

 > The total number of filtered stars is still pretty large! What would be a good way of architecting the data so it is quickly accessed, and a huge kd-tree with millions of nodes can be built in memory?

✦ To handle a dataset on the scale of the filtered Gaia catalog (which will still contain hundreds of millions of stars), you need to move away from "iterative" programming and adopt a Data-Oriented Design. 

  If you try to load 500 million stars into RAM and call insert() in a loop 500 million times, your program will likely grind to a halt due to memory fragmentation, cache misses, and continuous re-balancing overhead.

  Here is the best architectural approach to build and query a massive KD-Tree with extreme speed:

  1. Bulk Loading (Do not use insert)
  Never build a massive KD-Tree by inserting points one by one. 
  Instead, load all your coordinates into a single, flat array in memory, and pass them to a Bulk Build function (like kd_build in your C code, or build_tree in your Julia code). 

  Bulk builders work by finding the median of the entire array, splitting it in half, and recursively doing the same for the sub-arrays. 
   * Speed: It calculates the tree in O(N log N) time, which is exponentially faster than millions of individual insertions.
   * Perfect Balance: It guarantees the resulting tree is 100% perfectly balanced, meaning your search queries will always operate at maximum theoretical speed (O(log N)).

  2. Arena Allocation (Flatten the Tree in Memory)
  In languages like C or Go, allocating millions of individual tree nodes using standard malloc or new spreads the nodes randomly across your RAM. When you search the tree, the CPU constantly stalls waiting to fetch random memory
  addresses (Cache Misses).

  Instead, use an Arena Allocator (which is exactly how we designed your new Rust implementation!):
   1. Pre-allocate a single, massive array (e.g., nodes = malloc(500_000_000 * sizeof(Node))).
   2. Instead of using pointers (*Node) to link parents to children, use integer indices to point to positions in that array.
   3. Why this matters: Because the tree is stored sequentially in a contiguous block of RAM, the CPU cache will automatically pre-fetch the next nodes during a search, resulting in a 10x to 50x speedup in query times.

  3. "Sharding" the Sky (The Master-Worker Architecture)
  Even with Arena allocation, loading 500 million stars into a single tree requires a supercomputer with 64GB+ of RAM. To run this on standard hardware, you must "shard" (partition) the dataset.

  Because the Gaia catalog is already split into multiple .fits.gz files based on HEALPix sky sectors, you should exploit that:
   1. Worker Trees: Build a separate, standalone KD-Tree for each individual FITS file, and save that tree to disk.
   2. The Master Tree: Create one tiny "Master" KD-Tree in RAM. This Master tree doesn't contain stars; it contains the 3D bounding boxes of the 100+ different FITS files.
   3. Query Pipeline:
      * When you want to find stars near coordinate $X, Y, Z$, you first query the Master Tree.
      * The Master Tree instantly tells you: "That coordinate only intersects with File #12 and File #47".
      * Your program then only loads the pre-built Worker Trees for File 12 and 47 into RAM, executes the exact search, and combines the results.

  4. Memory Mapping (mmap)
  Once your Worker Trees are built, do not write code that "reads" the tree from disk into RAM. 

  Instead, write the raw bytes of your Arena Array directly to a binary file on disk. When your program starts, use Memory Mapping (mmap in C/Rust, Mmap.jl in Julia) to link that file directly to your CPU's virtual memory space. 
   * The OS will trick your program into thinking the entire 10GB tree is loaded into RAM instantly.
   * As you search the tree, the OS will only physically load the exact 4-kilobyte pages of the file that your search path actually touches. 
   * Startup time drops from 5 minutes to 0.001 seconds, and memory usage remains incredibly low!

✦ The names of the Gaia files (like GaiaSource_000000-003111.csv.gz) are not random at all—they represent a highly specific mathematical map of the sky!

  To make a 1.8-billion-star catalog manageable to download and process, the European Space Agency (ESA) didn't just dump all the stars into a single massive file. They chopped the sky up into tiny puzzle pieces using a geometric system
  called HEALPix (Hierarchical Equal Area isoLatitude Pixelization).

  Here is the significance of the filenames:

  1. The HEALPix Grid
  Imagine drawing a grid of diamonds all over a globe, where every single diamond covers the exact same amount of surface area. In the HEALPix system, the sky is divided into 12 massive base pixels. As you increase the "level" or
  "resolution," each pixel splits into 4 smaller pixels. 

  For the Gaia DR3 catalog files, ESA chopped the sky up at HEALPix Level 8. This divides the entire celestial sphere into exactly 196,608 equal-area pixels.

  2. The Filename Numbers
  In the filename GaiaSource_000000-003111.csv.gz:
   * 000000 is the starting HEALPix index.
   * 003111 is the ending HEALPix index.

  This specific file contains every single star that was observed inside the geometric boundary of pixels #0 through #3111. 

  Why is it organized this way?
  This is an incredibly powerful architectural choice for a few reasons:

   1. Spatial Locality: Because the files are grouped by their physical location in the sky, you don't have to download the entire 1.8-billion-star catalog if you only care about researching the Orion Nebula. You can just look up which
      HEALPix indices cover Orion, and download only the 2 or 3 files that map that area!
   2. File Size Balancing: Some parts of the sky (like the galactic plane of the Milky Way) are jam-packed with stars, while others are relatively empty. ESA groups the indices dynamically so that every single .csv.gz file contains
      roughly 500,000 stars, keeping the file sizes manageable and consistent. That's why one file might cover 3,000 empty pixels, while another file might only cover 5 densely-packed pixels.

  The KD-Tree "Sharding" Connection
  This HEALPix organization is exactly why the "Master-Worker" KD-Tree architecture I suggested earlier works so beautifully! 

  Because each file is already grouped spatially, the stars in GaiaSource_000000-003111.fits.gz are already physically clumped together in the same region of the universe. 

  You can build one separate KD-Tree for each file, and your "Master Tree" just needs to hold 3,386 bounding boxes (one for each file). When you query the Master Tree, it instantly routes you to the exact HEALPix file that contains your
  search coordinate, without ever needing to load the rest of the galaxy into RAM!

✦ This is a deeply insightful question, and the answer is a mix of "Yes" and "No," because of how the final catalog data is mathematically processed.

  To understand this, we first have to clarify where Gaia actually is. Gaia is not orbiting the Earth. It is parked at the Sun-Earth Lagrange Point 2 (L2), which is about 1.5 million kilometers "behind" the Earth. However, it still
  orbits the Sun exactly once a year, meaning it experiences the same orbital geometry as Earth.

  Here is how Earth's/Gaia's orbit affects the final numbers you see in the catalog:

  1. Parallax: Dependent on the Orbit
  Parallax is fundamentally caused by the movement of the Earth (and Gaia) around the Sun. 

  As Gaia orbits the Sun, the apparent position of a nearby star traces a tiny ellipse against the background sky. The parallax value you see in the Gaia catalog is the semi-major axis (half the widest part) of that apparent ellipse. 

  By definition, this measurement is standardized to a 1 Astronomical Unit (1 AU) baseline—which is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun. So, the parallax value is intimately and entirely dependent on the geometry of our orbit
  around the Sun.

  2. Right Ascension (RA) and Declination (Dec): Independent of the Orbit
  If you look at the raw data coming off Gaia's cameras on any given Tuesday, the star's apparent RA and Dec are affected by where Gaia currently is in its orbit. 

  However, the ra and dec values published in the final Gaia DR3 catalog are Barycentric.

  Before ESA publishes the data, they apply complex mathematics to completely remove the effects of the orbit. They shift the reference frame from the spacecraft to the Solar System Barycenter (the center of mass of the entire solar
  system, which is usually just outside the surface of the Sun). 

  Therefore, the ra and dec in the catalog are independent of the orbit. They represent the "true" direction of the star as if you were standing perfectly still at the center of the solar system at a specific reference epoch (for DR3,
  the epoch is exactly Julian Year 2016.0).

  3. The Hidden Complication: Stellar Aberration
  Even after correcting for the physical position in the orbit, astronomers also have to correct for the speed of the orbit. 

  Because Gaia is flying through space at ~30 kilometers per second, the light hitting its telescope behaves like raindrops hitting a moving car's windshield—it appears to be coming from slightly "in front" of the true source. This
  optical illusion is called Stellar Aberration. 

  ESA meticulously calculates the spacecraft's exact velocity at the millisecond the picture was taken and mathematically strips this distortion out of the data to find the star's true Barycentric RA and Dec.

  Summary
   * Parallax is the direct measurement of how our orbit affects our viewpoint.
   * RA and Dec in the catalog have been mathematically scrubbed clean of our orbit, giving you the pure, objective coordinates of the star relative to the center of the solar system.

✦ This is a great question that highlights the difference between how humans track time (civil calendars) and how physics tracks time (continuous scales).

  When astronomers say "Julian Year 2016.0," they are actually making two very specific, highly calculated choices: picking the year 2016.0, and using the Julian time system.

  Here is why both choices are necessary:

  1. Why the year 2016.0? (Minimizing Error)
  The coordinates (RA and Dec) of stars change constantly due to proper motion. To publish a catalog, you have to freeze the universe at one specific, universal microsecond and say: "This is exactly where everything was at this exact
  moment." That moment is called the Epoch.

  Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3) is built from 34 months of continuous observations, collected between July 2014 and May 2017. 

  If ESA set the epoch to the beginning of the mission (2014), the mathematical uncertainty of where the stars moved by the end of the mission (2017) would magnify. By setting the epoch to exactly mid-way through the observation period
  (January 1, 2016, or 2016.0), the statistical errors in the proper motion and positional calculations are perfectly balanced and minimized across the dataset. 

  (For comparison, Gaia DR2 used Epoch J2015.5, because it only included 22 months of data!)

  2. Why the "Julian" system? (The Math Problem)
  When astronomers use the word "Julian," they are not talking about the ancient Julian Calendar invented by Julius Caesar. They are referring to the Julian Date (JD) and Julian Year systems invented in the 1500s.

  The standard calendar we use on Earth (the Gregorian calendar) is a mathematical nightmare for physics:
   * Months have 28, 29, 30, or 31 days.
   * Years have 365 days, except every 4 years (366 days), except every 100 years, except every 400 years.
   * We randomly inject "leap seconds" into December or June to account for the Earth's rotation slowing down.

  If you want to calculate exactly where a star will be 14.7 years from now using its proper motion, you cannot plug Gregorian dates into a linear algebra equation.

  The Julian Solution:
  A Julian Date is simply a continuous, unbroken count of days (and fractions of a day) since noon on January 1, 4713 BC. 
  A Julian Year is defined in astrophysics as exactly, strictly 365.25 days (or exactly 31,557,600 seconds). No leap years, no leap seconds, no irregular months. 

  Because a Julian Year is a perfectly uniform metric, you can use basic linear equations to fast-forward or rewind the universe. If you want to know a star's Right Ascension in the year 2050, the math is effortlessly simple:
  RA₂₀₅₀ = RA_{2016.0} + \Big(ProperMotion × (2050.0 - 2016.0)\Big)

  Seasonal Drift and Julian Years: Explain the concept of seasonal drift in the Julian calendar versus the Julian Year (astronomical), and how leap years handle the drift.


✦ Yes, there is! But to explain how and why, we have to split the concept into two different things: the Civil Julian Calendar (invented by Julius Caesar) and the Astronomical Julian Year (used in physics). 

  Here is how drift affects both of them.

  1. The Civil Julian Calendar (Massive Drift)
  When Julius Caesar reformed the calendar in 45 BC, he instituted a rule: every year has 365 days, except every fourth year, which has 366 days (leap year). This meant the average length of a Julian calendar year was exactly 365.25
  days.

  The problem? The Earth’s actual orbit around the sun (the "Tropical Year", which dictates the seasons) takes 365.24219 days.

  The Julian calendar was overestimating the length of a year by about 11 minutes and 14 seconds. While 11 minutes doesn't sound like much, it compounds. Every 128 years, the calendar drifted by a full day.

  By the 1500s, the Julian calendar had drifted by 10 full days. The Spring Equinox (which was supposed to happen on March 21st) was occurring on March 11th. The seasons were physically desyncing from the dates on the calendar. 

  This is why Pope Gregory XIII introduced the Gregorian Calendar in 1582. He deleted 10 days from October to snap the seasons back into place, and tweaked the leap year rule (skipping leap years on century years unless divisible by 400)
  to ensure the drift would only equal 1 day every 3,236 years.

  2. The Astronomical Julian Year (Zero Drift)
  In astronomy and physics, the "Julian Year" (denoted by the symbol "a" or "j") is not a calendar. It is a rigidly defined unit of time, like a meter or a kilogram.

  An Astronomical Julian Year is defined as exactly 31,557,600 SI seconds. (Which works out to exactly 365.25 standard 24-hour days).

  Because it is a fixed unit of time, it does not "drift" in the mathematical sense. However, because it is strictly tied to 365.25 days, it does drift against the actual physical seasons of Earth. 

  If you used Astronomical Julian Years as your literal calendar, your dates would suffer the exact same 11-minute-per-year drift that Julius Caesar's calendar suffered, eventually moving winter into July. 

  But astronomers don't care about Earth's seasons! They only use the Julian Year to measure the decay of radioactive isotopes or the orbits of distant planets. When an astronomer says a star is 10 light-years away, they mean the light
  took exactly 10 Astronomical Julian Years to reach us—regardless of what season it was on Earth when the light departed.

  If you are measuring the half-life of Uranium-238, or calculating the orbital period of an exoplanet in the Andromeda galaxy, Earth's seasons are completely irrelevant.

 > From the data we calculated, is there a way to estimate the size of a star?                     
▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀▀
✦ Yes, absolutely! In astrophysics, we can estimate a star's physical size (radius, R, in terms of
  Solar Radii, R_\odot) solely from the data we have processed—namely its distance d (derived from
  parallax) and its apparent magnitudes ($G, BP, RP$)—using three fundamental astrophysical
  principles:

  ---

  Step 1: Calculate the Absolute Magnitude (M_G)
  Apparent magnitude (m_G) is how bright a star looks from Earth. Absolute magnitude (M_G) is how
  bright the star would be if placed at a standard distance of 10 parsecs. 
  Using the distance d (in parsecs):
  M_G = m_G - 5 log₁₀(d) + 5

  ---

  Step 2: Calculate Stellar Luminosity (L/L_\odot)
  Luminosity (L) is the total energy output of a star per second. We can estimate the star's
  luminosity in terms of the Sun's luminosity (L_\odot) by comparing the star's absolute magnitude
  to the Sun's absolute magnitude in the G-band (M_{G,\odot} ≈ 4.67):
  (L)/(L_\odot) = 10^{(4.67 - M_G)/(2.5)}

  ---

  Step 3: Estimate Effective Temperature (T_{eff})
  We can estimate a star's temperature from its Color Index, which is the difference between its
  blue and red band magnitudes:
  Color = BP - RP

  Using empirical color-to-temperature relationships for main-sequence stars, we can approximate
  the temperature (T_{eff} in Kelvin):
  T_{eff} ≈ 10^{3.979 - 0.20 · (BP - RP)}
  (Hotter blue stars have a low/negative color index and higher temperature; cooler red stars have
  a high color index and lower temperature.)

  ---

  Step 4: Calculate the Stellar Radius (R/R_\odot)
  According to the Stefan-Boltzmann Law, a star's luminosity is proportional to its surface area
  (4π R²) and the fourth power of its temperature (T⁴):
  L = 4π R² σ T⁴

  By dividing a star's equation by the Sun's equation (T_\odot ≈ 5,778 K), we get the scaling
  relation:
  (L)/(L_\odot) = \left((R)/(R_\odot)\right)² \left(\frac{T_{eff}}{T_\odot}\right)⁴

  Solving for the radius (R/R_\odot):
  (R)/(R_\odot) = √((L)/(L_\odot)) \left(\frac{5778}{T_{eff}}\right)²

  ---

  Example Calculation:
  If we load a nearby Sun-like star with d = 10 pc, G = $4.67$, and $BP-RP = 0.82$:
   1. M_G = 4.67 - 5log₁₀(10) + 5 = 4.67
   2. L/L_\odot = 10^{(4.67 - 4.67)/(2.5)} = 1.0 (Exactly 1 Solar Luminosity)
   3. T_{eff} = 10^{3.979 - 0.20 · (0.82)} ≈ 5,800 K (Almost identical to the Sun)
   4. R/R_\odot = √(1.0) · (5778 / 5800)² ≈ 1.0 (Exactly 1 Solar Radius)
